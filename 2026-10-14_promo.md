# Neologismata, Volume 1 — GitBook Launch: A Patristica Update

## Cold open: Hamilton and the science of new words

You know "Hamilton." Lin-Manuel Miranda's musical sets the American founding in the late eighteenth century, but tells it in a musical idiom that did not exist in the 1770s — hip-hop, R&B, pop. America then read by America now. But imagine for a moment that no scholarly record of "Hamilton" survived. Imagine that two thousand years from now, a researcher only has access to manuscripts that may date 100--300 years after Hamilton was first staged. Could that researcher date the document using nothing but the words on the page? The setting is the 1790s. But the vocabulary, if has the ears to hear it, sounds ... different.

To illustrate the point, I did some digging. Using Google's n-gram viewer,  a searchable database of all the words in millions of books spanning several centuries, I isolated over a dozen words in Hamilton that are clear markerse of late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century origins. Consider a sample:

- "okay" as an interjection — Hamilton himself says "Laurens, Mulligan, Marquis de Lafayette, okay, what else?" The word "okay" was not in circulation in the 1770s, but emerged in the 19th century and came to saturate American English in the 20th.
- "yo" as a greeting — "Yo yo yo yo yo! What time is it?"
- "hey" in the modern vocative — "Hey yo, I'm just like my country, I'm young, scrappy and hungry."
- "ya" as a possessive — "the truth is in ya face when ya hear the British cannons go."
- "Chicka-boom!" as onomatopoeia.
- "self-starter" — a twentieth-century coinage of American self-help culture: "by being a self-starter, by fourteen, they placed him in charge of a trading charter."
- "scammin'", "knucklehead", "code word", "horror show", "infighting", "deniability", "adrenaline" — every one of these can be pinned, with data, to a period long after the ostensible setting of the story.

We know these words, and some of them _sound_ modern, but how many did you know weren't circulating until recently? Now imagine how much 2000 years of hindsight might muddle the distinctive signals of a composition. Without the help of massive amounts of historical data, scholars might find Miranda's modern slang blending seamlessly into the American Revolution and conclude the play was composed in the mid-18th century without ever realizing they were at least a century off. 

Implicit setting is no evidence of date. The words themselves testify, if we build the instruments to hear them.

This what *Neologismata* is all about.

## The book

A neologism — a new word — creates a rippling in Time. It splashes forward upon its surface of porous fabric; echoes in waves, spoken and written, until it enters our ears and minds. Neologisms hide in plain sight, embedded among more commonplace words. Massive corpus linguistics projects are now changing how we detect them.

For ancient Greek, the Thesaurus Linguae Graecae now holds well over 125 million words, searchable by lemma and sortable by date. Add to that several open-access corpora, such as Leuven's GLAUx or Leipzig's Opera Graeca Adnotata, and with some scripting, database compiling, and expert evaluation of dates and sources, we can now build a data-magnet to detect neologistic needles hiding in these high and heavy lexical haystacks.

In this book, I took the end of Hadrian's reign in 138 CE as a chronological line of demarcation, then work through the non-Christian Greek authors of the second and early third centuries one at a time — Arrian, Ptolemy, Apollonius Dyscolus, Aelius Promotus, Favorinus, Aretaeus, Appian, Aelius Aristides, Lucian, Galen, Marcus Aurelius, Julius Pollux, and more, twenty-four chapters in all. The net result is over forty NT lemmas that apparently first emerged, or were first popularized, after Hadrian — tiny treasures, micro-features, trace elements that together give us our clearest picture yet of the dates when documents in the NT were composed, edited, and/or reached stable form.

To be sure, caution is warranted. Out of all the candidates proposed, undoubtedly some will be disproved as post-Hadrianic; I am cautiously optimistic the false positive rate will be 20% or less. The book's conclusion even catalogs the words I initially misidentified, later flushed by queries against papyri and inscriptions, since failures are often more valuable than successess in the history of scientific progress. But if even half of these anchors stand, that alone should decisively shift the Overton window on the compositional and redactional dates of the canonical New Testament.

## What's new: the gitbook

Which brings me to today's announcement. *Neologismata* is now available as a gitbook — a living, web-based edition of the full text, all twenty-four chapters, endnotes and bibliography included, free to read in any browser.

Why a gitbook? Because this kind of research is exactly the kind that demands many eyes. Every lemma I have dated is a claim about the entire surviving Greek corpus, and no single scholar commands that corpus. A papyrologist might surface a pre-Hadrianic attestation I missed — it has happened before, and I have flushed the false positive accordingly. A specialist in the Second Sophistic might challenge a date I have accepted. A reader with sharper database skills might run the queries I should have run. The gitbook format — versioned text, public change history, open contribution — is built for precisely this kind of collaborative sifting. Progress demands that we search as much data as are available, together.

So consider this an open invitation. If you work in Greek lexicography, corpus linguistics, patristics, papyrology, epigraphy, Second Sophistic literature, or New Testament studies — or if you are a student who has found a gap in one of my arguments — the gitbook is now your venue. Read it. Check my work against the corpora. Challenge, confirm, correct, and concretize.

I'll close with this. The book is free to download and read on Zenodo, and available in print for those who like a paper copy.
